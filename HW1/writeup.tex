\section{Introduction}
%=======================================================================

Bisection is guaranteed to converge but only linearly; Newton's method
converges quadratically but may diverge if started poorly. Problem 1 combines
the two into a hybrid scheme and uses it to find all ten roots on $[0,2]$ of
%
\begin{align}
f(x) = {}& 512x^{10} - 5120x^{9} + 21760x^{8} - 51200x^{7} + 72800x^{6} \nonumber \\
         & - 64064x^{5} + 34320x^{4} - 10560x^{3} + 1650x^{2} - 100x + 1 .
\label{eq:poly}
\end{align}
%
Problem 2 generalizes Newton's method to $d$ dimensions and applies it to
%
\begin{equation}
x^2 + y^4 + z^6 = 2 , \qquad
\cos\!\left(x y z^2\right) = x + y + z , \qquad
y^2 + z^3 = (x + y - z)^2 ,
\label{eq:sys}
\end{equation}
%
near $\mathbf{x}^{(0)} = (-1.00,\, 0.75,\, 1.50)$.

%=======================================================================
\section{Theory and numerical approach}
%=======================================================================

\subsection{Bisection}

If $f$ is continuous on $[a_0,b_0]$ with $f(a_0) f(b_0) < 0$, the interval
brackets a root. At each iteration the midpoint
$x_n = \tfrac{1}{2}(a_n + b_n)$ is formed and whichever half preserves the
sign change is retained. The bracket width obeys
$b_n - a_n = 2^{-n}(b_0 - a_0)$, so
%
\begin{equation}
|x_n - x^\star| \le 2^{-(n+1)}(b_0 - a_0) ,
\label{eq:biserr}
\end{equation}
%
i.e.\ linear convergence at $\log_{10} 2 \approx 0.30$ digits per step. Note
that \eqref{eq:biserr} bounds the error by a contracting envelope but does not
force it to decrease at every step, since the midpoint may land nearer the root
at one iteration than at the next.

\subsection{Newton's method}

Linearizing $f$ about $x_n$ and requiring the linear part to vanish gives
%
\begin{equation}
\delta_n = -\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} , \qquad x_{n+1} = x_n + \delta_n ,
\label{eq:newton}
\end{equation}
%
and expanding about the root yields
$e_{n+1} = \left[f''(x^\star)/2f'(x^\star)\right] e_n^2 + \mathcal{O}(e_n^3)$:
convergence is quadratic provided $f'(x^\star) \ne 0$ and $x_0$ is close
enough. In $d$ dimensions the same construction gives a linear system rather
than an explicit formula,
%
\begin{equation}
J(\mathbf{x}_n)\, \boldsymbol{\delta}_n = -\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_n) ,
\qquad \mathbf{x}_{n+1} = \mathbf{x}_n + \boldsymbol{\delta}_n ,
\qquad J_{ij} = \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j} ,
\label{eq:newtond}
\end{equation}
%
which is solved at each step with MATLAB's backslash operator rather than by
forming $J^{-1}$. Convergence remains quadratic near a root at which $J$ is
nonsingular. There is no $d$-dimensional analogue of a bracket, so nothing
plays the role bisection plays in Problem 1.

\subsection{Implementation}

\texttt{hybrid} bisects until $b_n - a_n \le \texttt{tol1}\cdot|x_n|$, then
takes the final midpoint as the starting point for \eqref{eq:newton}, iterating
until $|\delta_n| \le \texttt{tol2}\cdot|x_{n+1}|$. Both criteria are relative,
as specified, with a floor on the denominator to cover a root at the origin.
\texttt{newtond} applies \eqref{eq:newtond} until
$\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_n\| \le \texttt{tol}\cdot\|\mathbf{x}_{n+1}\|$. Both
routines carry iteration caps.

Initial brackets are found by brute force: $[0,2]$ is sampled at $N = 10001$
points and each adjacent pair with $f(x_i) f(x_{i+1}) < 0$ is recorded. The
spacing $\Delta x = 2\times 10^{-4}$ is far below the smallest root separation
($\approx 9.7 \times 10^{-2}$), so no root is missed. For Problem 2 the system
\eqref{eq:sys} is written in residual form and its Jacobian is hard-coded
analytically.

\subsection{Exact roots of the test polynomial}

The leading coefficient $512 = 2^9$ identifies \eqref{eq:poly} as a Chebyshev
polynomial, $f(x) = T_{10}(x-1)$. Since $T_{10}$ vanishes at
$\cos[(2k-1)\pi/20]$, the roots are known exactly,
%
\begin{equation}
x_k^\star = 1 + \cos\!\left(\frac{(2k-1)\pi}{20}\right) ,
\qquad k = 1, \dots, 10 ,
\label{eq:exact}
\end{equation}
%
all real, simple and in $[0,2]$, providing an independent check on the
results. No such closed form is available for \eqref{eq:sys}.

\section{Discussion and conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion}
%=======================================================================

Both routines performed as intended. The bisection phase reliably delivered an
estimate inside Newton's basin of convergence, after which Newton reached
$\texttt{tol2}$ in two or three steps---against the seventeen further halvings
bisection would have needed---and the $d$-dimensional solver converged to
\eqref{eq:root3d} with a machine-precision residual.

The one result that looks like a defect is the growth of $|f(x_k)|$ across
Table~\ref{tab:roots}, spanning seven orders of magnitude. Comparison with the
exact roots shows this is not a loss of accuracy in the roots themselves. Two
effects contribute. The residual and error are related by
$|f(x)| \approx |f'(x^\star)||x - x^\star|$, so a steeper root gives a larger
residual for the same positional accuracy; more importantly, evaluating
\eqref{eq:poly} near $x = 2$ sums terms of magnitude
$512 \times 2^{10} \approx 5\times10^5$ which must cancel to something of order
unity. That cancellation costs $\approx 5.7$ digits and imposes a floor of
order $10^{-16} \times 5\times10^5 \approx 10^{-10}$ on the computed value of
$f$, irrespective of how well $x$ is known. The observed residuals sit at that
floor, and the same conditioning explains the $10^{-10}$ disagreement with
\texttt{roots}. It also caps any useful choice of \texttt{tol2} at around
$10^{-12}$ for the outermost roots; a Chebyshev-basis representation would
avoid the problem entirely.

Problem 2 illustrates the fragility of Newton's method away from a root: the
relative update grows at $n = 4$ before recovering, so the supplied starting
point is close enough for eventual convergence but not for the quadratic law to
hold from the outset. This is exactly what bisection guards against in Problem
1, and no analogous safeguard exists in several dimensions. Damping the step
until the residual norm actually decreases would protect against the excursion
seen here.

The main difficulty was interpreting the residuals of Table~\ref{tab:roots}
before the identity $f(x) = T_{10}(x-1)$ was noticed; without an independent
reference it was unclear whether they indicated a genuine failure at the upper
end of the interval or an artifact of the function evaluation. No comparable
check exists for Problem 2, which is why the convergence history is reported:
absent an exact answer, an observed quadratic rate together with a
machine-precision residual is the strongest available evidence that the
iteration has found a genuine root.
